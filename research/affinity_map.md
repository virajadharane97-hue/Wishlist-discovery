# Affinity map
Observations from 20 written responses. One observation per note.
Group names are verbatim phrases taken from respondents, not the
author's terms.

---

## "all of them are about how pretty it looks" — 8 respondents
Reviews exist and are read, but they report appearance on arrival
rather than the thing the respondent needs to know.

- Reviews mostly cover size and delivery, not fabric or embroidery (R9)
- Nobody discusses long-term durability; reviews are written straight after delivery and say "good quality" without explaining what that means (R10)
- Reviews describe how the bag looks on receipt, not how it holds up over months (R11)
- Product description uses marketing language; no review from anyone using the shoe for the same activity (R12)
- Q&A answer to a length question was "true to size", which means nothing (R13)
- 11 reviews, all about how pretty it looks, not one says whether they were sweating in it (R14)
- Only 4 reviews and two are photographs of the packet (R16)
- Reviews are all about how nice it looks; the dimensions given are external, not internal (R17)

---

## "they're all 5'6+" — 4 respondents
The reference bodies available — models, reviewers, influencers — are
never close enough to the respondent's own to transfer.

- Size chart gives measurements but not how the pants sit on an actual body; reviewers rarely state their height or measurements (R7)
- Checks the model's measurements first because the professionally photographed version isn't the actual fit (R11)
- Reviews have photos but nobody mentions their height, or if they do they're all 5'6+ (R13)
- Wants a real person wearing it, ideally someone with her body type (R14)

---

## "One photo. That's the entire blocker" — 5 respondents
The gap between saved and bought is a single specific artefact, not
persuasion, price or redesign.

- One full-length review photo with the reviewer's height stated, or the inseam number in the listing (R13)
- Even one video of a real person, ideally someone with her body type — "I'm one nudge away, honestly" (R14)
- One real photo of the fabric up close, or one video review (R16)
- A photo of the bag with a laptop inside it — "One photo. That's the entire blocker and it's slightly absurd that it doesn't exist" (R17)
- Two sentences of measurements in reply to a DM (R18)

---

## "Three people said runs small so I ordered L. It was too big." — 1 respondent
The workaround was executed correctly and still failed.

- Measured with a tape, compared to the chart, was between M and L, read reviews sorted by size, three reviewers said it runs small, ordered L, it was too big, returned it and gave up on the dress (R13)

---

## "Slightly ridiculous but it worked" — 5 respondents
Resolving an online purchase by going offline, or by spending
substantial time searching outside the listing.

- Checked the store (R1)
- Didn't order; went for offline shopping instead (R4)
- May visit the physical store and try the brand's clothes before ordering online, for expensive items (R12)
- Went to the Zara store at Phoenix Mall specifically to try the size, didn't buy there, ordered online later for the colour (R14)
- Went to the Uniqlo store at BKC on a Saturday to try the size, then bought online because the store lacked the colour; won't order online without having tried the brand's fit (R17)
- Went through a brand's tagged Instagram photos to find a real person, found one who stated 5'10 wearing M, ordered M at 5'11 — "took me about 30 minutes of scrolling to figure that out" (R18)

---

## "I don't really trust only the pictures on the website" — 4 respondents
The imagery itself is suspected of misrepresenting the product.

- Most available content is posted by the brand or by influencers who were sent the product (R8)
- Lighting changes the colour quite a lot in the few customer photos that exist (R9)
- Meesho photos are clearly stolen from other websites — the model and background differ across photos of the same product (R16)
- The base looks narrower than the top in the photos, so the stated width might be the widest point (R17)

---

## "different brands have different fittings" — 4 respondents
Size labels are not portable across brands, so past experience does
not transfer.

- Size fit differs across brands for the same person (R5)
- Whether the shoulders will be too tight, as different brands size differently for the same person (R6)
- If normally an M in Zara, what would that be in this brand (R18)
- Compares the size chart against clothes already owned rather than trusting the label (R12)

---

## "there's nothing else to go on" — 3 respondents
The information does not exist anywhere, not merely in the wrong form.

- No size chart at all, only S/M/L in the caption; DM unanswered after four days; other people asking the same question in comments with no answers; 8k followers and no reviews anywhere (R18)
- Size chart gives waist and hip but no inseam (R13)
- Product page states the outer fabric but says nothing about the lining (R14)

---

## "half shopping list and half reference folder and the app has no idea which is which" — 6 respondents
The wishlist holds two incompatible kinds of item, and nothing
distinguishes them.

- Wishlist is basically a storage place for things they might want someday (R10)
- Some saves are inspiration — saved for the styling, colour or silhouette, with no intention of owning that item (R11)
- The wishlist is "kind of my mood board" (R13)
- It's "basically my Pinterest, I scroll it when I'm bored", and clearing it would be a loss (R16)
- Saved purely for the aesthetic, not to own (R18)
- Things that looked amazing in a reel but don't suit her lifestyle, kept because she still likes the styling (R8)

---

## "deleting them feels like admitting I'm not going to buy them" — 4 respondents
Removing an item carries a cost, so dead saves accumulate.

- Keeps forgotten and impulse saves because deleting them feels unnecessary (R7)
- Hasn't deleted items whose occasion has passed because she still likes the designs (R9)
- Deleting feels like admitting she isn't going to buy them (R14)
- Aspirational bags kept as "a reminder of what I'm working towards, which sounds silly written down" (R17)

---

## "not in my budget" — 5 respondents
Price is the stated blocker, with no information need attached.

- Price to be reduced (R1)
- More saving (R2)
- Liked it but it's not in her budget (R3)
- Very good offers (R5)
- Expensive stuff saved to see the price again (R15)

---

## "I don't necessarily need a lower price" — 3 respondents
Price explicitly rejected as the solution, unprompted, in a question
that had already instructed them to assume constant price.

- "At this price I don't need a huge amount of information, just some confidence that it's not poor quality" (R10)
- "I don't necessarily need a lower price" (R11)
- "I don't need a discount; I just want to know that it's the right type of shoe for what I need" (R12)

---

## Ungrouped observations

- Called the Hidesign store in Palladium; the assistant said she'd check and never called back (R17). Sits between the offline workaround group and the information-does-not-exist group.
- Asics changes the model number every year and the listing doesn't say what differs from the previous version (R15). Product identity rather than fit, fabric or appearance; no other respondent raises it.
- A pair of shoes out of stock in her size for months (R14). Availability, which is out of scope by the scope decision recorded in the brief.
- "Honestly nothing this week. Maybe if my current shoes fully broke" (R15). Need-triggered rather than information-triggered; the only respondent for whom no artefact would help.
- Would buy if she found nothing better elsewhere (R20). Comparison against the wider market rather than a blocker on the item itself.

## Note on group sizes

# Addendum: groups from Q14 and Q15

---

## "myntra cargo pants honest review" — 8 respondents
The search string itself shows what they are looking for. Almost every search contains the word review, and several qualify it. They are not searching for the product. They are searching for another person's experience of it.

- "black cargo pants myntra review fit" and "myntra cargo pants honest review" (R7)
- "[brand] reviews dress" and "is [brand] legit" (R8)
- "pastel green kurta set myntra review", plus sent the product screenshot to her sister (R9)
- "best cotton oversized tshirt under 700" and "myntra tshirt quality review" (R10)
- "[bag] long term review" and "[brand] quality review" (R11)
- "[shoe model] walking review" and "[shoe model] running review" (R12)
- "olive wide leg trousers petite" — searched for a shorter version rather than a review (R13)
- "hidesign [model] laptop fit review" (R17)

Note the qualifiers: honest, long term, walking, laptop fit. The generic review is not what they want. They want a review from a specific use case or a specific duration.

---

## "too much for a mehendi??" — 4 respondents
The item is sent to a specific person with the question attached. These are the exact messages.

- Sent the product screenshot to her sister asking if she liked it (R9)
- Sent the link to her sister on WhatsApp: "too much for a mehendi??" — sister replied "it's nice but where will you wear it again" (R14)
- Sent the screenshot to a friends group: "649 cargo, worth risking?" — one replied "meesho cargo is always shiny don't" (R16)
- DMed the brand: "hi, could you share chest and length measurements for M?" — no reply after four days. Also searched the brand on r/IndianFashionAddicts, nothing came up (R18)

Two of these workarounds produced a no. The sister introduced an objection the respondent had not raised — where will you wear it again — and the friend advised against the purchase outright. Asking a trusted person resolves the uncertainty; it does not reliably resolve it in favour of buying.

---

## "whether I want to gamble ₹649 or spend ₹1,199 safely" — 3 respondents
The comparison is not between products. It is between a cheaper item with unknown fit and a more expensive item with known fit. These respondents are willing to pay a premium to remove the uncertainty.

- A similar item from H&M at ₹1,999, which is more expensive, but "I've bought H&M pants before so I know my size there. Basically deciding between cheaper-but-risky and pricier-but-safe." (R13)
- One on Myntra at ₹1,199, almost double the ₹649 option, "but Myntra returns actually work. Deciding basically on whether I want to gamble ₹649 or spend ₹1,199 safely." (R16)
- A Da Milano bag at ₹11,000 "that I know fits because I've seen a colleague's", against a ₹3,500 alternative she does not want. Deciding on longevity. (R17)

---

## Comparison behaviour, Q15 — 11 of 20 hold alternatives
Nine respondents said they were not comparing anything. Of the eleven who were, the attributes compared are almost never price alone.

- Fit, fabric, reviews, and whether alterations would be needed (R7)
- Overall look, fabric, reviews and return policy, "more than just price" (R8)
- Colour and embroidery, wanting elegant rather than heavily embellished (R9)
- Price, fabric composition, ratings and number of reviews (R10)
- Capacity, material, how professional they look, and whether they work with both Indian and western outfits (R11)
- Cushioning, weight, durability, and whether appropriate for her actual use "rather than just choosing based on appearance" (R12)
- Cheaper-but-risky against pricier-but-safe (R13)
- A more occasion-appropriate option that is less flattering, against a cheaper one of visibly poorer quality (R14)
- Gambling on the cheap option against paying double for working returns (R16)
- Longevity: a known-fitting expensive bag against a cheaper one she does not want (R17)

One respondent has no alternative, and says that is the problem: "the exact silhouette isn't available from bigger brands. Closest is an H&M one that's much more fitted, which defeats the point." (R18)
